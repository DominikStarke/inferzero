A river town keeps its records in three places. The first is the ledger at the mill, where every sack of grain that
comes in by cart or by boat is written down with its weight, the farm it came from and the day it arrived. The miller
checks the scale each morning against a set of iron weights that the town bought forty years ago and keeps in a locked
box. When the scale and the weights disagree, nothing is ground until the scale is mended, because a wrong scale would
make every line of the ledger wrong at once, and nobody could tell afterwards which lines to trust.

The second place is the school, where the children copy the ledger's totals into their own books at the end of each
week. They add the columns by hand, then add them again in the other direction, top to bottom and bottom to top, and
the teacher only accepts a page when both sums agree. The children grumble that the second sum is a waste of time. The
teacher answers that a sum done twice the same way repeats its mistakes, while a sum done two ways catches them, and
that the town has more than once found a careless line in the mill's ledger this way.

The third place is the bridge keeper's house. Every boat that passes under the bridge pays a small toll, and the keeper
writes down its name, its cargo and the hour. On a busy day the keeper cannot write as fast as the boats arrive, so he
ties a knot in a cord for each boat and writes the entries in the evening, counting the knots to be sure that none was
forgotten. Once a storm soaked his book and the ink ran. He rebuilt the day from the cord, the mill's ledger and the
memories of the boatmen, and the three sources agreed on every boat but one, which turned out to have passed twice.

In spring the river rises. The mill stops when the water reaches the third step of the stairs by the wheel, because
above that mark the current is too strong and the gears would strip their teeth. The stop is written in the ledger
with the height of the water, so that the farmers know why their grain waits. The town has tried, over the years, to
let the mill run a little longer each spring, and it learned to change one thing at a time: a new gear one year, a
stronger axle the next, and each change was watched through a whole flood before the next one was made.

A traveller once asked the miller why the town did not simply buy a larger wheel and grind twice as much. The miller
took him to the wheel and showed him the marks on the stone where the old wheels had stood, each one a little wider
than the one before. A larger wheel, he said, needs a faster race, a faster race wears the banks, and worn banks flood
the lower fields. Every part of the mill is tied to the river and to the fields, so a change that helps the wheel can
hurt the farms. The town decides such changes together, after measuring what the river does with the old wheel and
what it would do with the new one, and it writes the measurements down so that the next generation can check them.

The children like the bridge best. In summer they count the boats from the railing and compare their count with the
keeper's book. Most days the counts agree. On the days they do not, the keeper and the children go through the cord
knot by knot until they find the boat that one of them missed, and the keeper writes the reason next to the entry: a
boat that passed at dusk, a raft tied behind a barge, a fisherman who rowed under the arch without stopping. Over the
years these notes became a little book of their own, and the teacher reads from it when a child asks why a careful
count can still be wrong.

At the end of every year the three records are laid side by side on the long table in the town hall. The mill's
grain, the school's totals and the bridge's boats must tell the same story: grain that came by boat must have passed
the bridge, and every total in the children's books must match the ledger's. Where they agree, the year is closed.
Where they disagree, the town does not guess. It goes back to the scale, the sums and the cord, finds the one place
where the story split in two, and writes down what happened there, so that the mistake is not made again.
